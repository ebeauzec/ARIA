## Roles: what CSM, TAM, and SAM each own

These three roles overlap but have distinct centers of gravity — useful to be explicit about which hat is on before building a plan, since the phased plan in the playbook pulls different levers depending on which role (or blend) is driving it.

- **CSM (Customer Success Manager)** — owns *adoption and value realization* of what the customer has already bought. Center of gravity: are they actually using the product to get the outcome they bought it for, is usage trending up or down, is there a renewal/expansion risk forming. Primary tools: customer success plan, health score, QBR cadence, adoption tracking.
- **TAM (Technical Account Manager)** — owns the *technical relationship*: proactive risk reduction (upgrade planning, AutoSupport/Active IQ health, architecture review), escalation management, and being the trusted technical voice the customer calls before something breaks, not just after. NetApp sells this directly as a paid offering — see below. Primary tools: technical health reviews, upgrade/EOA roadmap, escalation runbook, architecture recommendations tied into [Operations-Troubleshooting](../Operations-Troubleshooting/README.md) and [Platforms-Hardware](../Platforms-Hardware/README.md).
- **SAM (Strategic/Sales Account Manager)** — owns *commercial growth*: pipeline generation, whitespace/expansion identification, competitive displacement, and closing net-new or expansion business. Primary tools: account plan, MEDDPICC-qualified opportunities, whitespace map against the NetApp portfolio.

In practice a single engagement plan usually blends all three — a TAM-flagged upgrade risk becomes a CSM-tracked adoption/health item and a SAM-qualified expansion opportunity (e.g., "customer is on an aging AFF A-series approaching EOA" is simultaneously a technical risk, a success-plan action item, and a Keystone/next-gen-platform pipeline opportunity).

## Customer success planning — frameworks

- **TSIA Customer Success Maturity Model** — [A Brief Guide to Customer Success](https://www.tsia.com/blog/a-brief-guide-to-customer-success) · [The Customer Success Maturity Model in 4 Phases](https://www.tsia.com/blog/4-phases-of-customer-success-maturity) · [Customer Success: 5 FAQs](https://www.tsia.com/blog/customer-success-5-frequently-asked-questions). TSIA frames customer success as **time-, event-, and value-based interactions** across the customer lifecycle, with a 4-phase maturity progression from reactive account-assignment-and-monitoring ("CS 1.0" — assign a CSM, watch a health score, intervene when risk appears) toward proactive, outcome-driven engagement integrated with support/training/services. Useful for self-assessing where a given account relationship currently sits before proposing a plan that assumes more maturity than exists.
- **Gainsight Success Plan structure** — [Success Plan Overview](https://support.gainsight.com/gainsight_nxt/Success_Plans/About/Success_Plan_Overview) · [Guide and Grow your Customers with Success Plans](https://www.gainsight.com/blog/guide-and-grow-your-customers-with-success-plans/). Industry-standard structure: a success plan defines the customer's **strategic goals**, tracks **objectives** (each with owner, task list, target outcome, due date), and is reviewed on a cadence (Gainsight's own tooling structures this as Plan Info / Cockpit (active actions) / Gantt / Timeline). The [Engagement-Plan-Playbook.md](Engagement-Plan-Playbook.md) template in this category is a tool-agnostic adaptation of this structure.
- **Customer health scoring** — [Gainsight: Customer Health Score Explained](https://www.gainsight.com/blog/customer-health-scores/). Practical guidance: score on **4–6 signals that actually correlate with renewal/expansion outcomes for the specific account base**, not a generic template — common inputs are product usage frequency/depth, support ticket volume and severity trend, engagement with enablement/training content, NPS/CSAT, and contract/renewal timing. Roughly 70–80% of accounts that eventually churn show measurable warning signs 30+ days before cancellation, with usage decline the single strongest leading indicator — meaning a TAM-visible signal (declining AutoSupport-derived usage, deferred upgrades, unresolved P1/P2 case patterns — see [Operations-Troubleshooting](../Operations-Troubleshooting/README.md)) is often the earliest real warning, ahead of anything visible from the commercial side alone.
- **QBR (Quarterly Business Review) practice**: treat a QBR as a value-delivery checkpoint, not a status meeting — go in with a point of view on what's working, what's at risk, and a specific proposed next step (an adoption gap to close, an upgrade to plan, an expansion use case to pilot), rather than a recap of tickets closed.

## Sales pipeline generation & opportunity qualification — frameworks

- **MEDDIC / MEDDPICC** — [MEDDIC vs MEDDPICC](https://www.prolifiq.com/post/meddic-vs-meddpicc) · [MEDDPICC Implementation Guide](https://www.demandfarm.com/blog/meddpicc-sales-methodology/) · [Force Management: MEDDIC vs. MEDDPIC](https://www.forcemanagement.com/blog/meddic-vs.-meddpic-the-meaning-difference-and-benefits-of-each-for-sales-qualification-force-management). An evidence-based opportunity-qualification framework — qualify (and keep re-qualifying through the deal cycle) against: **M**etrics (the quantified business outcome the customer cares about), **E**conomic buyer (who actually controls/releases budget), **D**ecision criteria, **D**ecision process, **I**dentify pain (the compelling business reason to act now), **C**hampion (an internal advocate with real influence, not just enthusiasm), plus MEDDPICC's two additions: **P**aper process (procurement/legal/security-review steps that can stall a technically-won deal) and **C**ompetition. Sales teams using a formal qualification methodology report materially higher win rates than ad hoc pursuit — the value is in forcing an honest answer to "do we actually have a champion and an economic buyer identified" before investing further cycles.
- **Stakeholder / Power-Interest mapping** — [Power-Interest Matrix](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/stakeholder-mapping-power-interest-matrix/) · [Stakeholder Mapping for Complex Enterprise Sales](https://www.hyperbound.ai/blog/stakeholder-mapping-enterprise-sales). Four quadrants: **high power/high interest** (manage closely — champions, key decision-makers), **high power/low interest** (keep satisfied — executives/financial gatekeepers, engage with high-level ROI summaries not deep technical detail), **low power/high interest** (keep informed — end users/technical advocates, nurture with case studies and roadmap visibility), **low power/low interest** (monitor only). Named roles worth identifying explicitly in any account: economic buyer, executive sponsor, champion, technical buyer/evaluator, gatekeeper (controls access — EAs, chiefs of staff), and signer (legal contract authority — not always the same person as the economic buyer in larger orgs).
- **Challenger Sale** — [Salesforce: Challenger Sales Methodology](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/sales/challenger-sales-methodology/) · [Challenger Inc: SAP and the Challenger Way](https://challengerinc.com/blog/sap-and-the-challenger-way-of-selling/). Core move: **teach** the customer something new and commercially relevant about their own business/market (not just pitch features), **tailor** the message to their specific stakeholders, and **take control** of the sales process rather than being purely reactive to their stated requirements. Important caveat found this pass: Challenger's "disrupt the status quo" move works best on **net-new** logos with no existing relationship — applying a hard-challenge posture to an **existing account renewal/expansion** conversation has been shown to correlate with *lower* net revenue retention. For NetApp's largely install-base-heavy SAM motion, lead with Challenger-style insight on **net-new competitive displacement** pursuits; lead with a **value-realization/QBR-grounded** conversation (not a challenge) for expansion within an existing account.
- **Whitespace / account planning** — the discipline of mapping what a customer has already bought against the full available portfolio to find the gap ("whitespace"). This library's own category list *is* a ready-made whitespace map for NetApp specifically — see the next section.
